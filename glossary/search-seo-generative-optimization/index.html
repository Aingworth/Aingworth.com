Search, SEO & Generative Optimization

The established disciplines brands use to improve discoverability. Useful, and often necessary, but tactics that sit on top of a brand's underlying authority infrastructure rather than replace it.

Search Engine Optimization (SEO)

Improving a site so search engines can index, understand, and rank it. Industry Term

Search Engine Optimization, or SEO, is the practice of improving a website so that traditional search engines can index, understand, and rank it. It remains the technical foundation that generative and answer engines build on.

Why this matters

AI features sit on top of the same index as ordinary search. A page that cannot be crawled or understood is not eligible for either, which is why the fundamentals have not gone away.

Generative Engine Optimization (GEO)

Optimizing content for representation in generative AI environments. Industry Term

Generative Engine Optimization, or GEO, refers to practices intended to improve how a brand is represented within generative AI and answer-engine environments. GEO can be a useful tactical discipline, but sustainable recognition depends on the authority infrastructure behind the content, not only on optimizing it for an AI interface.

Why this matters

GEO describes a set of tactics, not a position. Treating it as the whole strategy leaves a brand optimizing content for an interface while the authority underneath it stays thin.

Answer Engine Optimization (AEO)

Structuring content so answer engines can extract and use it. Industry Term

Answer Engine Optimization, or AEO, refers to structuring content so that answer engines can understand, extract, and use it when responding to a question. It is a useful tactical discipline within a broader recognition strategy, not a replacement for one.

Why this matters

Making content extractable is genuinely useful work. It just determines how easily a system can use a source, not whether it trusts it enough to want to.

Semantic Search

Search that interprets meaning rather than matching keywords. Technical Term

Semantic search is a search approach that interprets the meaning and intent behind a query, rather than matching keywords alone.

Why this matters

It is why keyword density stopped working. Systems now match on meaning, which rewards clear, well-defined language over repetition.

RelatedEmbeddingsSemantic OptimizationVector Search

Semantic Optimization

Structuring content so its meaning, not just its keywords, is clear. Industry Term

Semantic optimization is the practice of structuring content so its meaning, not just its keywords, is clear to search and AI systems, including the entities, relationships, and context involved.

Why this matters

It is the practical response to semantic search: be explicit about what you mean, who you are talking about, and how the ideas relate.

Entity Recognition

Identifying and classifying real-world things within text. Technical Term

Entity recognition is the process by which a system identifies and classifies real-world things, such as a person, brand, or place, within text or data.

Why this matters

Before a system can attach any authority to a brand, it has to reliably pick that brand out of text and tell it apart from similarly named ones.

RelatedEntityEntity ConsistencyKnowledge Graph

Entity Authority

The strength and consistency of the signals tied to an entity. Industry Term

Entity authority describes the strength and consistency of the signals connecting an entity to its identity, expertise, relationships, and reputation across the digital ecosystem. Strong entity signals help systems distinguish who a brand is and what it is known for.

Why this matters

It is the technical vocabulary closest to Earned Authority, and a useful bridge when talking to search teams who think in entities rather than in brand terms.

Knowledge Graph

A structured map of entities and how they relate. Technical Term

A knowledge graph is a structured database of entities and the relationships between them, used by search and AI systems to understand how people, brands, and concepts connect.

Why this matters

Presence in a knowledge graph is one of the strongest forms of structural authority, because it is where a system stores what it considers settled about an entity.

RelatedStructural AuthorityEntityEntity Recognition

Structured Data

Information formatted so machines can read it directly. Technical Term

Structured data is information organized in a standardized format so machines can read and interpret it directly, rather than inferring it from unstructured text.

Why this matters

It removes guesswork. Anything a brand leaves unstructured, a system has to infer, and inference is where misrepresentation starts.

Schema Markup

A structured data vocabulary added to a page to disambiguate it. Technical Term

Schema markup is a specific vocabulary of structured data, typically added to a webpage, that helps search engines and AI systems understand what the page is about and disambiguate its entities.

Why this matters

Schema is infrastructure, not a citation trick. It helps systems tell one entity from another, which is a prerequisite for recognition rather than a shortcut to it.

RelatedStructured DataStructural AuthorityEntity Recognition

E-E-A-T

Google's Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, and Trustworthiness framework. Industry Term

E-E-A-T is Google's framework for evaluating content quality: Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, and Trustworthiness. It is established search guidance, and it connects directly to the broader authority signals AI systems draw on, though Aingworth does not treat it as a separate, AI-specific standard.

Why this matters

It is Google's own published guidance, which makes it useful and checkable. It is worth naming accurately rather than restating as an AI framework it was never written as.
